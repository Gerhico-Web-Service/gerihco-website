AI Risk Frameworks: Turning Guidance into Decisions

Before an AI system goes live, leaders need more than a risk rating. They need evidence that it is fit for its intended use, a clear account of remaining exposure, and agreed conditions for restricting or stopping it. A risk framework helps structure those decisions by connecting potential impacts, technical testing, and operational controls. Its value lies in the action it informs—not the label it provides. Adopting a framework does not, by itself, establish compliance or guarantee safety.

Choose a framework for the decisions it supports

The value of a risk framework is not its name on a policy cover. It is the consistency it brings to decisions about whether an AI use is appropriate, what evidence is needed, and when deployment should be restricted or stopped. Begin with organizational objectives and applicable obligations. Then connect AI assessments to existing enterprise risk, information security, privacy, procurement, change management, and incident response processes rather than creating a disconnected compliance exercise.

Understand the different roles of established resources

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) 1.0 is a voluntary resource organized around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. Its Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile (NIST AI 600-1) addresses risks associated with generative AI. ISO/IEC 42001:2023 specifies requirements for an AI management system; ISO/IEC 23894:2023 provides guidance on AI-related risk management. These resources serve complementary purposes, but using them does not by itself establish legal or contractual compliance. Frameworks evolve, so confirm the applicable versions when setting policy or approving a deployment.

Turn risk categories into concrete scenarios

An entry such as inaccurate output is too broad to guide a control decision. Describe the event, its cause, the affected process, and the consequence: for example, a document assistant invents a requirement that a reviewer mistakenly includes in an operating procedure. Consider data leakage, biased outcomes, malicious instructions embedded in retrieved material, unreliable sources, supplier changes, and excessive agent permissions. Evaluate how failures could combine, including cases where a persuasive explanation causes a reviewer to miss an error.

Require evidence matched to the use case

Set evaluation methods and acceptance criteria before testing. Include representative tasks, difficult exceptions, and relevant misuse scenarios, and record limitations in the data and methods. Assess output quality alongside access controls, data handling, human review, and recovery arrangements. Document the risk before controls, the controls applied, the remaining exposure, and the person authorized to accept it. A rating without evidence or an owner responsible for addressing the risk is a label—not a management decision.

Keep the assessment alive

Start with a clear evidence trail for each AI use case. Record risks, tests, controls, approvals, and monitoring, and reassess material changes. Use incidents and complaints to improve safeguards, with clear escalation, rollback, and suspension criteria. The goal is informed decisions throughout the lifecycle—not a one-time approval.
