People, AI Agents, and Automation: Designing the Future Workforce

Giving an AI agent a task is also a decision about authority: what it may access, what it may change, and when a person must step in. Designing work around people, AI agents, and automation starts with those boundaries—not with the technology alone. A practical operating model pairs defined tasks and controlled permissions with employee involvement, meaningful review, and a workable fallback. The aim is better service and work quality, with responsibility remaining with people.

Redesign tasks before redefining jobs

Here, non-human workforce refers to software agents, AI models, and automated processes—not independent decision-makers with human responsibilities. Start by mapping the work: what information enters, what judgment is required, what actions follow, and who is accountable for mistakes. Distinguish assistance from recommendation and execution. Permission to summarize a document should not automatically confer authority to update a record, contact a customer, or commit organizational resources.

Assign an accountable owner to every agent

Treat each agent as a controlled system with a defined purpose, an accountable owner, a distinct system identity, and a managed lifecycle. Limit access to what its assigned task requires; avoid shared credentials and unrestricted tools. For agents that take action, define approval boundaries, appropriate transaction limits, and activity records. When an agent is retired or reassigned, revoke unneeded access and apply approved data-retention requirements.

Make human oversight meaningful

Adding a person to a process does not automatically create an effective safeguard. Review becomes a formality if that person cannot inspect the evidence, challenge a recommendation, or interrupt execution. Specify when review occurs, what must be checked, and which conditions require escalation. Test a workable manual fallback. For higher-consequence workflows, use checks sufficiently independent of the system being reviewed; another agent may reproduce the same errors.

Invest in the people doing the work

Employees closest to a process can help identify exceptions, undocumented dependencies, and practical failure modes. Involve them in workflow design and explain what is changing, what remains their responsibility, and how concerns can be raised. Training should cover data handling, verification, limitations, and incident reporting—not only effective prompting. Preserve opportunities to practice essential skills so that the organization retains the expertise needed to challenge automation and operate when it is unavailable.

Measure the complete outcome

Start with one defined workflow and a documented baseline for comparison. Measure quality, errors, rework, accessibility, supervision, and workload alongside speed. Test human oversight and fallback arrangements, and expand only when evidence supports it. The goal is better work with clear accountability—not simply more automation.
